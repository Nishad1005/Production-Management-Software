How to read this

33 actions, grouped by screen in the order of the menu. Each one has the steps in words, a picture before with the control numbered, the dialog if there is one, and a picture after with what changed numbered. Under the pictures: what moved, and why the software works that way.

These pictures are of the demonstration build, where every figure is invented so that every screen has something on it. The controls are the same on the live system; the numbers are not. Two things exist only there: the sign-in page, and the Users screen where roles are given.

Command centre

1Run the scheduleCommand centre
  1. Choose which orders to plan with the three buttons.
  2. Press Run the schedule.
  3. Wait for the stamp beside the title to change.
What changed

A new plan was written as its own version (10 OCT 2026 · 1453 MS). Every order was planned again from nothing: 102 jobs, 12 that do not fit — the same figures, because nothing had changed since the last run.

Why it matters

Every run is kept and none is overwritten, so pressing this is never destructive. It is the one button that turns everything on Masters, the Capacity sheet and the Order book into dates.

Attention

2Follow a finding to the screen that fixes itAttention
  1. Read the card. The first line says what is wrong; the second says why and when it ships.
  2. Press "Go to the screen that fixes this".
What changed

"Order Linen, natural now — the date has passed" opened Order now, or it will be late, the screen where that kind of problem is handled.

Why it matters

A finding with nowhere to go is a complaint. Every card on Attention names the screen that fixes it, and there is deliberately no way to dismiss one while it is still true.

Load heatmap

3Open one day of one departmentLoad heatmap
  1. Find the square: one row per department, one square per working day, red when the day is over-full.
  2. Click it.
What changed

A panel opened below the map listing the 3 jobs on Assembly that day, with the share of the day each one takes. Together they come to 300%.

Why it matters

The heatmap shows which days hurt; this shows what is on them. A department can be making several things at once, and the shares add even when the pieces do not.

Schedule

4Show one department, and only the problemsSchedule
  1. Pick a department from the first list.
  2. Tick "Breaches only".
  3. Press Expand all to see the bars.
What changed

8 shipment lines became 8 with stitching work, then 2 with a stitching breach: 2 red bars once opened.

Why it matters

With hundreds of live orders the full schedule is unreadable. A department head wants their own rows; a planner wants the red ones.

5Drag a bar to pin a job to a dateSchedule
  1. Open a shipment line, then press on a bar and drag it left or right. The days move as you drag.
  2. Let go. A dialog asks why.
  3. Type the reason and press Pin it.
What changed

The job was fixed to the date it was dropped on, the plan re-ran around it, and a "Manual pins" panel now lists it with the reason "Pulled forward to protect the December container". Every later run will honour the pin until it is released.

Why it matters

The engine does not know about a customer visit or a machine arriving on Tuesday. A pin is how a planner overrules it, and the reason is what stops that becoming a mystery in six weeks.

6Release a pinSchedule
  1. Find the pin in the "Manual pins" panel.
  2. Press Release.
What changed

The pin was removed and the plan re-ran. The panel still lists the other pins.

Why it matters

A pin is a decision with a date on it. When the reason has passed, releasing it hands the job back to the engine.

Order book

7Add an orderOrder book
  1. Press Add an order.
  2. Enter the ERP number, the customer, the article, the quantity, the stuffing date and the confidence.
  3. Press Add order.
What changed

The book went from 8 orders to 9, and the schedule ran immediately: the new line shows no breaches before anyone has looked at it.

Why it matters

An order is planned the moment it exists. Nobody has to remember to re-plan, and the first thing anyone sees about a new order is whether it fits.

8Open an order to see its shipment linesOrder book
  1. Click the order’s row.
What changed

SO/26-27/0412 opened to show its shipment lines, each with a quantity, a container reference, a stuffing date and a confidence. The row offers "Add a shipment line" and a way to delete the order.

Why it matters

An order is not one dated promise. It ships in phases, and each phase is planned backwards from its own container. The shipment line is the unit the whole system works in.

Accept an order

9Ask whether an order can be taken — and hear noAccept an order
  1. Choose the article, type the quantity and the stuffing date.
  2. Press Check and wait: the whole factory is planned again with this line added, then the line is removed.
What changed

"9 of 12 steps cannot be made to this date." Every department is listed with the dates it would need; the ones that fail say why. Nothing was added to the order book.

Why it matters

This is the question that matters before anyone promises a buyer. The answer comes from the same engine as the real plan, against everything already committed.

10Ask again with a quantity that fitsAccept an order
  1. The same screen, with 40 pieces for 10 February 2027.
  2. Press Check.
What changed

"Every department can make this within its window." The panel title reads "Yes".

Why it matters

The same check, the other answer. A merchandiser can try quantities and dates until one fits, and only then promise it.

What if

11Try a change on a copy of the planWhat if
  1. Name the scenario.
  2. Choose the department and the change. Department down means no output at all; overtime adds a fifth; a second shift doubles.
  3. Press Run it and wait about a minute on the live system, a second or two here.
What changed

Breaches went from 12 in the live plan to 19 in the scenario, and 9 jobs moved. The live plan was not touched.

Why it matters

Taking a department out for a fortnight cannot make the plan better, and the screen says exactly how much worse. The same lever answers "would a second shift fix it?" before anyone is hired.

12Make a scenario the planWhat if
  1. Run a scenario, or press Compare on one in the Runs list.
  2. Press Make this the plan.
What changed

The scenario "Overtime on stitching" became the live plan. Every screen now reads from it. The plan it replaced is still in the Runs list and can be compared or brought back.

Why it matters

Deciding is a separate step from trying. Nothing becomes the plan until somebody says so, and the previous plan is never lost.

WIP

13Open an order in progress to see its routeWIP
  1. Click the order card under "In progress".
What changed

The card opened to show the route department by department: what each was asked for, what it declared, and what the next department counted in. The line is 21% of the way through.

Why it matters

Counted, not valued: every figure here is something a department declared against what the plan asked for. It is where "where is my order?" is answered without a phone call.

My department

14Choose a department and read its dayMy department
  1. Pick a department from the list.
What changed

Stapling’s board: what it is waiting for from Foam Pasting and Stitching, then what it owes, with late work separated from work not yet due.

Why it matters

A department head needs one list: what can I start, and what am I holding up. The feeders are named from the route, so nobody has to know the whole factory to read it.

Production

15Write down what was madeProduction
  1. Choose the department and the day. If the day has no work, the screen offers the days that do.
  2. Type the good pieces, and any rejected, against each job.
  3. Press Enter, or Save.
What changed

Stitching declared 26 good pieces against one job. The button reads "Entered". Dates did not move: declaring output never changes the plan, it records what happened against it.

Why it matters

Two minutes a day per department is what brings WIP, the dashboard, quality and the forecast to life. Good and rejected are counted separately; the percentage is worked out, never typed.

16Count in what the previous department handed overProduction
  1. Choose the receiving department. Anything the previous department declared and this one has not counted sits at the top.
  2. Type what actually arrived.
  3. Press Count in.
What changed

Stitching had declared 26; stapling counted 24. The shortfall of 2 is kept against the handover rather than smoothed away, and the item left the queue.

Why it matters

Two-sided counting is what makes WIP believable. One department’s claim is not the record; the next department’s count is.

17Say who came in, and watch the day’s capacity followProduction
  1. Under "What you can do today", type how many people are in on the shift.
  2. Press Save.
What changed

Five in against a sanctioned crew moved the day’s capacity in proportion (WHO CAME IN · 11 of 12 in — the day is running at 92% of the rate. 1 of 7 rates have no crew size and are unaffected.). The plan re-ran with that day shorter.

Why it matters

The standing rate assumes the crew it was measured with. Half the people means half the day, and the engine should know that before the day is planned, not after it is missed.

Manpower

18Mark one person outManpower
  1. Choose the department.
  2. Find the person and press Out (or On leave).
What changed

Shabana Ansari is marked out. Stitching’s head count went from 11 to 10 without anyone typing a total, and the Production screen’s "what you can do today" reads the same figure.

Why it matters

Attendance is recorded once, per person, and the department’s number is derived from it. Two ways to say how many came in is how a wrong number ends up looking normal.

Material

19Count a material in the storeMaterial
  1. Under "Against the store", click the On hand figure on a row.
  2. Type the count and press Enter.
What changed

PLY-18 went from "not counted" to "covered". Counting the store did not re-run the schedule: it changes whether the plan can be met, which is a different question from what the plan is.

Why it matters

"Nobody has counted it" and "there is none" are kept apart on purpose. A material that is short raises a warning; one that is past its ordering date raises a critical finding; one that is uncounted says so.

Money

20Open an article’s cost sheetMoney
  1. Under article costs, click an article that shows a breakdown.
What changed

The Betsy chair opened into its cost lines: leather, foam, labour and the rest, summing to the unit cost that the cash-out panel uses.

Why it matters

Cash out by week is only as honest as the costs behind it. The panel says what it cannot price rather than totalling only what it can.

Capacity sheet

21Switch between rate, crew and day-count; find one articleCapacity sheet
  1. Press one of the three views.
  2. Type part of a code or a name to show one article.
What changed

The grid switched to day-counts and narrowed to the Betsy chair. Each cell is one article in one department; a blank means the article does not go there.

Why it matters

One number per cell, because three across a thousand cells is not something anyone can read. This is the grid every other number in the system is arithmetic on.

22Enter a rate, and route an article through a departmentCapacity sheet
  1. Click a blank cell under Units per day.
  2. Type the rate and press Enter.
What changed

A rate of 42 routed 125034299 through MACHINE: pairings went from 76 to 77, and the schedule re-ran with a new job in it.

Why it matters

A units figure means "this article passes through here at this speed". Clearing it removes the step. The rate is what the department makes in a day doing nothing else, which is what lets several products share a day.

23Enter a day-count the route contradicts, and correct itCapacity sheet
  1. Under D-minus, click sanding’s cell and type 70. Assembly feeds sanding and is at D-64, so sanding would be due before assembly had finished.
  2. Read the warning panel that appears above the grid.
  3. Type 56 again.
What changed

The warning "A department is due before something that feeds it" appeared, marked "Causing breaches": sanding at D-70 against assembly at D-64. Correcting the figure cleared it rather than leaving it to be dismissed.

Why it matters

The software will not guess which of the two numbers is wrong. While the contradiction stands, the engine holds the work behind something not yet due and reports breaches that are not real, so it says so loudly.

24Give an article a cost, and read it on the dashboardCapacity sheet
  1. Find the article and click "cost" in its row header.
  2. Type the unit cost and press Enter.
  3. Open the Dashboard.
What changed

A cost of 16,760 on the article in progress gave the dashboard a WIP value (WIP VALUE ₹661,584 all 1 lines in progress). Before it, the tile said what it was waiting for rather than showing a zero.

Why it matters

WIP value is the figure an MD quotes first and the one most easily invented. It is computed only from articles that have a cost, and it says how many lines it covers.

Masters

25Change a department’s yieldMasters
  1. Under Production route, click the yield figure on a department.
  2. Type the new percentage and press Enter.
What changed

Stitching’s yield went from 96% to 95% and the schedule re-ran: every department before stitching now has to make a little more, so that enough survives. (Put back to 96% afterwards.)

Why it matters

Losses compound down the route. A yield is not a note about a department; it changes how many pieces every earlier department is asked for.

26Change what feeds whatMasters
  1. Under What feeds what, find the row of the department that waits and the column of the one it waits for.
  2. Click the cell to toggle the link.
What changed

The link from assembly to sanding was removed and the plan re-ran: sanding no longer had to wait for assembly, so a runway breach that depended on that link went with it. The factory map redraws from the same grid. (Put back afterwards.)

Why it matters

The route is a graph, not a line. Departments that nothing connects run in parallel, and a false link holds work behind something it never waited for.

27Switch a second shift on for a departmentMasters
  1. Under Shifts, press Switch on beside the shift.
  2. Under Who works which shift, click Not running on the department.
What changed

Stitching now runs two shifts. Its rates on shift A were copied across, so the department’s day roughly doubled and the plan re-ran. The headcount beneath can be edited.

Why it matters

Capacity is per department per shift. The copied rates are a starting point and will be wrong if the second shift is staffed differently; a pairing switched on with no rates at all is flagged in red.

28Edit the D-minus matrixMasters
  1. Under D-minus matrix, click a cell.
  2. Type the number of days before stuffing and press Enter. Clearing it leaves the cell blank, and that article stops scheduling until it is filled.
What changed

D-80 became D-84: that department must now finish four working days earlier on that article, and the schedule re-ran. A blank cell blocks scheduling rather than defaulting to zero.

Why it matters

This is the same grid as the Capacity sheet’s D-minus view, kept here beside the route. A silent zero would produce an impossible schedule that looks entirely normal on screen.

29Declare a holidayMasters
  1. Under Holidays, press Add a holiday.
  2. Enter the date and what it is (here 11 November, stock-taking), and press Add holiday.
What changed

The day closed. Every working-day count in the system was renumbered, so a job due 20 working days before stuffing now starts a day earlier if the holiday falls inside its window. Sundays are already closed and are not listed.

Why it matters

D-minus is counted in working days. A holiday is not a note on a calendar; it moves every date that counts across it.

30Add an article, and take it to schedulableMasters
  1. Under Articles, press Add an article and fill in code, name and category.
  2. On the Capacity sheet, give it a rate in one department.
  3. Switch to D-minus and give it a day-count in the same department.
What changed

The article went through three honest states: "no route" (nowhere to be made), "D-minus missing" (routed, but nobody has said when the step must finish), and schedulable. It was never quietly planned on a zero.

Why it matters

Every refusal here is deliberate. An article that scheduled with a missing figure would produce a plan that looks normal and is wrong, which is the worst outcome available.

31Book a machine downMasters
  1. Under Machines, press Book downtime.
  2. Choose the machine, say why and for how long, and press Book it.
What changed

Stitching went from 7 of 8 machines to 6 of 8, and the department’s capacity on those days fell in proportion when the plan re-ran.

Why it matters

A machine going down is not a note. It moves capacity, and the plan should know before the day is missed.

32Save the masters to a file, and load them backMasters
  1. Press Save masters to a file. A file downloads.
  2. Press Load from a file and choose it.
What changed

The file came back and the screen reported "471 rows applied, and the schedule re-run.". Loading merges by code and never wipes what is already there, so a partly filled file is safe to apply.

Why it matters

This is how real figures move between systems and the door PPC’s workbook goes through. "Save everything" is the copy U&M control; it is held, not loaded back casually, because replaying production entries is how a factory gets a day it made twice.

Floor display

33Point the wall screen at a departmentFloor display
  1. Open the display address on the television’s browser.
  2. Choose the department in the small list at the bottom. It is remembered after a power cut.
What changed

The screen switched to Stitching: what is due today or overdue, what it is waiting on, who is in, and machines running. No menu, no chrome.

Why it matters

A wall display is not an application window. Nobody ten feet away reads a navigation bar, and nobody should have to touch it after a power cut.
