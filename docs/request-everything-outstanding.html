What it does not have is a single real number from your factory. Every rate, every deadline, every crew size and every order currently in the system was invented by us so that the screens had something to say. The system labels them as placeholders on every page, and they will be replaced the moment yours arrive.

01 The capacity workbook The single blocker
From
PPC
Format
The Excel workbook attached — same layout it is read back in, nothing retyped

Seventy articles across fourteen departments is 980 cells. Each one wants three numbers:

NumberWhat it meansWhat it decides in Kram
Units per day How many of that article that department finishes in a day The whole capacity model. Every heatmap cell, every over-capacity flag, every “can we take this order” answer.
Manpower The crew size that figure was measured with Whether marking someone absent changes the day’s capacity at all.
D-minus How many days before the container stuffing date that department must be finished Every deadline. The schedule is built backwards from the container through these.
The one rule that decides whether the numbers work

“Units per day” must mean that article and nothing else — what the department would do in a full day if it made only that item. Not its share of a normal mixed day.

Kram adds up the fractions of a day each item consumes, not the items themselves — you cannot add legs to covers, but you can add the hours they take. A department that enters its blended everyday figure will appear to be asked for three days of work every day, and will show up as your bottleneck when it is not.

A partly-filled sheet is genuinely useful

Fill in what you know and leave the rest. It can come back more than once as figures firm up, and each time it comes back it arrives carrying the previous round’s answers already in place.

If you do only one thing: fill in a single article completely, across all fourteen departments. That alone proves the whole chain end to end and tells us within a day whether the conventions above have been understood the same way on both sides.

Three other things we assumed, which you may want to correct

  • Yield: 98% at every one of the fourteen departments. This decides how much extra each must make to cover losses.
  • Working days: Sundays off, and no other holidays. Your holiday list would replace this.
  • Departmental headcount, which is what overtime and extra-people figures are calculated against.
02 What feeds what Everything is read through this
From
PPC, or whoever runs the floor
Format
A list of pairs, or fifteen minutes on a call

Kram does not assume departments run in a straight line. Where two departments do not depend on each other they run in parallel, which is what makes the plan realistic — and to do that it needs to know which department genuinely waits for which.

We reconstructed this from your concept deck and it is almost certainly right. It has never been confirmed by anyone at U&M, and every deadline in the system hangs off it.

One question we can settle today: does Machining feed Ply Cutting?

Our current graph says Machining must finish before Ply Cutting starts. Everything else about the route says the opposite — Ply Cutting is first in the sequence and is scheduled to finish four days before Machining.

Those two statements contradict each other, and Kram is currently reporting that contradiction as 70 critical warnings, one for every article, plus twelve of its eighteen “cannot be made as planned” findings. It is one line in a table. It is also the smallest possible version of this whole question — if you can answer it in a sentence, you can fill in the rest.

The two we were least sure of

  • Does Foam Pasting really wait for the wood finish to dry?
  • Do Cutting and Fibre Processing genuinely wait for nothing at all?
It is not set in stone

This is a grid on the Masters screen, editable by a planner at any time. Change one cell, re-run the plan, and every date moves with it. We are asking for your best current answer, not a permanent one.

03 One real export file out of Panipuri Longest lead time
From
Whoever runs Panipuri
Format
Whatever it exports — Excel or CSV, exactly as it comes out, unedited

Orders should arrive in Kram from Panipuri rather than being typed in twice. The import is ready to build and takes a few days — but written against a guessed column layout it will simply be written twice. One real file, even an old one with a handful of orders on it, removes that risk entirely. Please do not clean it up: the messy version is the useful one.

The question that decides the whole workflow

Does Panipuri hold a container stuffing date at all, or only a customer delivery date?

Kram schedules backwards from the stuffing date. If Panipuri holds only a delivery date, then somebody at U&M is working out the stuffing date today — and we need to know who, and how, because that calculation has to live somewhere in the system.

This does not block go-live. Orders can be entered by hand from day one. It is on this list because it is the item with the longest lead time and the least effort on your side.

04 Bills of materials, and a cost per article Two finished screens are idle
From
Purchase, and whoever holds costing
Format
A spreadsheet is fine. Start with one article.

Two screens are built, tested and completely empty because nothing has been entered against them:

  • Material — what the plan commits you to buying, week by week, and which items are already past the date they should have been ordered. It needs a bill of materials: what each article consumes, and how much.
  • Money — cash out against the plan over time. It needs a cost per article, and it will refuse to estimate one rather than print a figure nobody can stand behind.

One article’s worth of either is enough to switch the screen on and let you judge whether it is telling you something worth having.

05 Who should see what Needed before anyone gets a login
From
Management
Format
Names and email addresses, plus one decision

Kram has twelve roles — MD, planner, merchandiser, department head, HR, purchase, store, quality, maintenance, accounts, admin, and a read-only floor display. Access is enforced in the database itself, so it holds however the system is reached.

Today every role that can sign in can read everything. That is deliberate for a demonstration and wrong for a factory, and the answer is yours rather than ours:

  • Should maintenance see the customer order book?
  • Should store see commercial quantities and values?
  • Should a department head see other departments’ figures, or only their own?

Alongside that: the names and email addresses of the people who should have accounts, and which role each should hold. We create the accounts; roles are then assigned on screen and can be changed at any time.

06 A floor plan of the building Improves one screen
From
Anyone holding the drawing
Format
PDF, CAD, or a photograph of the printed plan

The Factory map today draws the route — departments arranged by what feeds what, coloured live by how loaded each one is. It says so on the screen, because inventing a building layout would produce a picture that is wrong in a way that looks authoritative.

With a real plan, the same live colouring can be laid over the actual shop floor, which is what makes it useful on a wall.

One thing for your compliance adviser, not for us

The overtime ceiling in the system is configurable and currently set to five hours on top of an eight-hour net shift, which is the figure the specification carries.

That is long against the Factories Act’s daily and quarterly limits, and multi-shift working carries its own provisions. Your compliance adviser should confirm the number before go-live. We flag it; we do not advise on it.
