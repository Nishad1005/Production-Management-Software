Kram works. What it does not yet have is your figures. Every rate, every yield, every deadline and every crew size in the version you have seen is invented — chosen so the screens had something to say. This note is about replacing them, and there is a workbook with it.

The workbook Two sheets

Attached is a blank sheet in the shape you already work in — every department across the top, every article down the side. Fill in what you know and leave the rest; a partly-filled sheet is genuinely useful, and it can be sent back more than once as figures firm up.

SheetWhat goes in it
Capacity Two columns per department — Manpower and Units. A blank pair means the article does not go through that department at all, which is the usual answer.
D-minus One number per department per article: how many days before the container stuffing date that department must be finished.

Send it back however suits you. It loads into Kram in one step, and loading it again later corrects what changed rather than duplicating anything — so an early, incomplete sheet costs nothing.

Three things worth being precise about

“Units per day” means this article and nothing else

We need what a department produces in a day if it works on only that article all day — not what it makes on a normal day with several articles running through it.

This is the one figure most likely to come back in the wrong form, and the consequences are not obvious. Kram adds up the fractions of a day each article consumes; that only works if each figure is a whole day's output for one article. Give it the everyday mixed number and a department will look like the bottleneck when it is not, and the software will warn about days that are perfectly fine.

Manpower is the crew that figure was measured with

If stitching makes 30 covers a day with three people, we need both numbers. That is the only way Kram can say anything useful when two of the three are absent — it scales the day to 20 because it knows the three. Without the crew size, attendance changes nothing and the plan carries on as though everybody came in.

D-minus is the anchor, and it has one rule

Every date in Kram is counted backwards from the container stuffing date. D-minus is how far back each department sits. A blank one stops that article being scheduled at all — deliberately, because treating a missing deadline as zero would produce a plan that looks entirely normal and is impossible.

The rule: a department must finish earlier than anything that depends on it. If Stitching feeds Stapling, Stitching's D-minus must be the larger number. Kram checks this as you type, names both departments when they contradict, and will not guess which of the two is wrong — that is your call.

Not in the workbook, but still needed Four short answers
WhatWhy we are asking
Yield per department The proportion that survives each operation. All fourteen are set to 98% at the moment. It compounds backwards, so it is not cosmetic — five departments at 98% each cost roughly a tenth of the factory.
Shifts and headcount Which shifts each department runs, and how many people are on the books for each.
Shift hours The net production hours — time actually available for work, excluding breaks, setup and cleanup — not the clock span. And the overtime ceiling per person.
Factory holidays For the next twelve months. Sundays we work out; declared holidays we cannot guess.

What we need back

The workbook with whatever you can fill in, the four answers above, and the what-feeds-what table from our previous note. In that order of usefulness — but the route table first if you only have time for one thing, because everything else is read through it.

Until these arrive Kram is planning a factory that resembles yours and is not yours. Every deadline it reports, every department it calls the bottleneck, and every warning it raises is only as right as these numbers.
