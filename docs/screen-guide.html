How to read the tags

Every screen below carries one of four tags. Together they answer the question this document exists for: which pages expect something from our side?

TagMeaning
Set-up inputNeeds data from U&M once (then only when things change): rates, routes, parts lists, costs, people. Until it arrives, the screen runs on our placeholder figures or sits empty and says so.
Daily inputNeeds U&M's people to type into it as part of the working day — this is where the software learns what actually happened.
Planner's toolInteractive, but nothing to maintain: a planner asks a question or makes a decision, the software answers or obeys.
OutputPure display. Nobody enters anything here, ever — it is the software's own conclusions from the inputs above.

There is also a twenty-first, the Floor display — a full-screen version of a department's day for a TV on the wall. Nobody touches it, so it is not in the menu and not counted here.

The short answer to "what do your people have to do": five screens need data set up once (the capacity sheet above all), and three need someone's two minutes a day — supervisors writing down production, someone marking attendance, and merchandising entering orders as they arrive. Everything else is the software talking back.

Words that appear everywhere

Learn these nine and every screen reads easily.

TermWhat it means
ArticleOne product in your catalogue — one SKU. There are 71 on the live system, and they are yours.
ComponentOne department's piece of an article — the stitching work on a chair is one component, the ply-cut parts another. Departments make components; containers ship articles.
Shipment lineThe unit everything is planned in. One quantity of one article going into one container on one date. An order shipping in two containers is two shipment lines — two separate deadlines, planned separately.
Stuffing dateThe day the container is loaded. Every date in the system is calculated backwards from this one.
D-minusHow many days before the stuffing date a department must finish its part. Stitching at D-26 must finish 26 days before the container. These numbers come from your planning team, per article per department.
RateHow many units of one article a department can make in a day doing nothing else. This "dedicated" wording matters: it is not the department's share of a normal mixed day, and entering it that way is the single most damaging mistake possible in this system.
UtilisationHow much of a department's day is spoken for, as a fraction. A department making three products at once adds the share of the day each takes: 0.4 + 0.4 + 0.4 = 1.20 — more work than the day holds. Anything over 1.00 is flagged.
YieldThe percentage of pieces that survive a department without being rejected. At 98%, a department must be given slightly more than the order needs. Losses compound down the route, so early departments make the most extra.
RunOne complete re-planning of the factory. Every run is kept forever and none is ever overwritten, so any old plan can be pulled back and compared with what really happened.
Command centre The whole plan, and the button that makes it Planner's tool

The planner's home page. The Run the schedule button re-plans the whole factory (about 70 seconds; every run is kept), and the rest of the page summarises the result.

TermWhat it means
Include ordersWhich promises to plan for. Confirmed only — signed orders. Confirmed + probable — adds orders you expect to sign; the usual choice. Everything — adds speculative forecasts too.
Shipment linesHow many container-loads the current plan covers (see the everywhere-words above — the line, not the order, is the unit).
Scheduled tasksThe jobs the plan breaks into: one task = one shipment line × one department × one component. 12 lines through 14 departments makes 168 tasks.
BreachesTasks that cannot be done as planned — the arithmetic does not fit. Each carries a reason (defined under the Schedule screen below): not enough working days, no material in time, no rate entered, and so on.
Flagged daysDepartment-days where more than a full day's work has been placed — utilisation over 1.00. Six flagged days might be one department six times or six departments once.
Bottleneck utilisationThe table ranking departments by how loaded they are on average. The top one is the factory's real constraint — extra capacity anywhere else changes nothing, so this is where the next person or machine goes.
AverageThe department's mean utilisation across the whole plan. 0.46 = a little under half of every day spoken for, on average.
PeakIts single worst day. 2.00 = one day holding two days' work.
FlaggedHow many of its days are over 1.00.
IdleHow many of its days have nothing planned at all. High idle next to a high peak means the work is bunched, not excessive — a re-sequencing problem, not a hiring one.
ConstraintThe label on the bottleneck — the highest average utilisation in the factory.
Flag triageThe overloaded days as a to-do list, sorted by how much warning remains.
Over byHow far past a full day that department-day is. +57% = the day holds 1.57 days of work.
Still possibleWhat the remaining notice still allows, from arithmetic on lead time: 45+ days out, hiring is still an option; 15–45, overtime / re-sequence / subcontract; under 15, honestly, a customer conversation. A label, never an instruction.
Attention Everything needing an answer, in one list Output

Every conclusion the other screens reach, gathered into one list of plain sentences and sorted by urgency. Each line links to the screen that fixes it. There is deliberately no way to dismiss an item — an alert you can silence while it is still true stops being read.

TermWhat it means
Needs an answer todayThe red items: work that cannot be made in time with under 15 days' notice, material past its last ordering date, a contradiction in the route, a department with nobody rostered.
Worth knowingThe amber items: overloaded days further out, material short against stock, machines down, articles that cannot yet be planned.
For the recordInformational: chiefly handovers one department has declared that the next has not yet counted in.
The red count in the headerThe number beside "things need an answer today", visible on every screen — it is this list's red items, following you around.
Dashboard The MD's nine indicators Output

One page for the morning question: is the factory on track today? Nine tiles, and a table of planned-against-made by department. A tile that cannot be computed honestly says what it is waiting for rather than showing a made-up figure.

TermWhat it means
Orders runningOrders currently open or in production.
Delayed ordersOrders whose plan already carries a breach — something in them cannot be made as scheduled.
Containers readyShipment lines where every department has finished — ready to stuff.
On time, in fullOf recently finished lines, the share completed by their date at full quantity.
Daily productionGood pieces declared across the factory, recent days.
Production efficiencyMade against planned, as a percentage, from the floor's own declarations.
RejectionsRejected pieces as a share of everything declared.
Material shortagesMaterials the plan needs more of than the store holds. Empty until parts lists arrive.
WIP valueThe rupee value of unfinished work. Deliberately refuses to display until article costs are entered — it is the number an MD would quote first, and the only one that would have had to be invented.
Planned / Made / VariancePer department per day: what the plan asked for, what was declared, and the difference.
Factory map The factory, as it flows Output

Each department as a box, arrows showing what feeds what, each box coloured by its worst day in the next fortnight and carrying its work-in-progress count. Drawn from the route your team confirms — it is a flow map, and says so; lay a real floor plan under it once one is supplied.

TermWhat it means
Part loadedLight green — some work, room to spare (utilisation up to about 0.8).
At capacityDark green — effectively full.
Over capacityRed — more than a day's work on the day. These are the flagged days.
IdleEmpty cell on a working day — nothing planned.
ClosedHatched — Sunday or a declared holiday. No work is ever planned onto one.
Share of dayIn the click-through panel: the fraction of that day one order's work takes. The cell's colour is these shares added up.
Capacity / PlannedAlso in the panel: what the department could make of that component that day, against what the plan asks of it.
Schedule Every job on a calendar Planner's tool

A bar per task, placed by working backwards from each stuffing date through the D-minus offsets. Red bars are breaches. Drag a bar to pin it.

TermWhat it means
Starts / DueThe first day of the job, and the day it must be finished (stuffing date minus its D-minus, moved to the nearest working day).
Must makeQuantity for the task — the order quantity grossed up for the yield of every department after this one, so enough survives.
PinA planner's decision that a bar stays where it was dragged. The plan reshuffles around it, it survives every future run, and it must carry a written reason (Why — required) so the decision can be accounted for later.
Manual pinsThe list of every standing pin, with its reason, and the release control.
Breach reasonsRunway — fewer working days than the department needs; overtime cannot fix this. Material — the work would have to start before material arrives. No capacity — no rate entered for this component in this department. D-minus missing — the offset was never entered, so it cannot be scheduled at all. Out of horizon — the dates fall outside the working-day calendar. Pin — a manual pin has pushed this past its due date; reported, never overridden.
Order book Every promise the plan is built on Daily input
TermWhat it means
ERP orderThe order's number in Panipuri, so the two systems always agree on which order is which.
ConfidenceConfirmed — signed. Probable — expected. Forecast — speculative. The planner chooses which levels each run plans for.
Lines / LineThe shipment lines under the order — one per container.
Stuffing / First stuffingEach line's container date; the order row shows its earliest.
Customer deliveryThe date the customer expects — recorded, but never planned from. Plans come from the stuffing date.
Material readyOptional: the day material will be available. Work is never scheduled before it, and a line that cannot fit after it becomes a Material breach.
Container referenceFree text naming the container, for the floor's benefit.
Not yet on a shipment lineOrder quantity not yet assigned to any container — unplanned until it is.
BreachesPer order: how many of its tasks the current plan cannot honour.
What U&M provides
Merchandising enters each order as it is confirmed — a minute's typing. Once a sample export from Panipuri is supplied and the import is built, this becomes a file upload instead.
Accept an order Can we say yes? Planner's tool

Enter an article, a quantity and a stuffing date. Kram plans it provisionally alongside everything already promised, reports the result, and throws the trial away — nothing joins the order book by checking. It genuinely re-plans the factory, so the answer takes about 70 seconds.

TermWhat it means
VerdictPer department: fits, or the breach reason it would cause.
Must make / Starts / DueAs on the Schedule screen — the trial order's own tasks, shown so the answer can be argued with rather than taken on faith.
What if Try a change on a copy Planner's tool
TermWhat it means
ScenarioThe change being tried: one department's capacity scaled up or down between two dates — four more people in stitching for a fortnight, or a week's shutdown.
What are you trying?A required label for the scenario, so runs can be told apart later.
Breaches / flagged days — now vs in this scenarioThe before-and-after, side by side. The live plan is untouched.
Now starts → thenPer task that moved: its start date under the live plan against its start under the scenario.
WIP Where everything is, right now Output
TermWhat it means
Work in progressAnything started and not finished — counted from the floor's declarations, not from the plan.
Ready to stuffLines where every department on the route has declared its full quantity.
In progressPer line per department: declared good pieces against required — "Stitching: 120 of 184".
Still to beginLines no department has declared anything against yet.
My department One department's day Output
TermWhat it means
What you oweThis department's tasks due soonest, quantities and dates, from the current plan.
Holding you upWhat this department is still waiting to receive from the departments that feed it — with late marked. The first place to look when a day stalls.
Production Writing down what was made Daily input

The screen the whole system learns from, built thumb-sized so it can be used standing on the floor.

TermWhat it means
What you were asked forToday's planned jobs for the chosen department.
Good / RejectedWhat the day actually produced: pieces that passed, pieces that failed (with the defect cause, which feeds the Quality screen). Declaring the same day twice corrects it rather than double-counting.
Handed to you, not yet counted inQuantities the previous department says it sent, awaiting this department's acceptance.
They made / You receivedThe two sides of a handover. They may differ — that difference is transit loss or a miscount, and the software records it instead of assuming either side is right.
What U&M provides
Two minutes per department per shift: good, rejected, and accepting what arrived. This single habit powers the Dashboard, WIP, Quality and Forecast screens.
Manpower Who came in — and capacity follows Daily input
TermWhat it means
Who is inEach department's people marked present, absent or on leave. A rate measured with 22 people scales down honestly when 19 come in — today's capacity moves everywhere in the software.
SanctionedThe established crew for a department on a shift — the denominator attendance is measured against.
DeploymentWhere people are against where the plan needs them today.
OvertimeRecorded as hours actually worked, per person — so extra capacity is real, never assumed. The ceiling per day is configurable and awaits your compliance adviser's confirmation.
Where overtime would close the gapOverloaded days where the shortfall is small enough that lawful overtime covers it — and, by omission, the days where it will not.
What U&M provides
Attendance marked daily (department head or HR), overtime hours as worked. Set-up: the sanctioned crew per department, in the capacity workbook.
Material What to buy, and by when Set-up input
TermWhat it means
NeededHow much of a material the current plan consumes, from the parts lists.
On hand / CountedThe store's last stock figure, and when it was counted — so an old count is visibly old.
Short byNeeded minus on hand, where positive.
First neededThe first production date that consumes it.
Order now, or it will be lateMaterials past their last safe ordering date — first needed, minus the supplier's lead time. The red half of the screen.
What U&M provides
Set-up: a bill of materials per article (what it consumes, how much), suppliers and lead times. Ongoing: stock counts from the store. One article's parts list switches the screen on for that article.
Quality Where the losses come from Output
TermWhat it means
Yield, countedThe yield the floor's declarations actually show — good over good-plus-rejected.
Against planCounted yield versus the yield the master claims for that department. The gap is the finding.
Biggest cause / ExplainedThe leading defect cause, and how much of the rejection total has a cause recorded at all — an unexplained balance is shown, not hidden.

Nothing is entered here; it is fed entirely by the rejections and causes written on the Production screen.

Forecast What the factory really achieves Output
TermWhat it means
How much of this is worth believingAlways first: how many days of real history exist against the ten observations each figure needs before it will speak.
Too few to sayThe refusal. Under ten observations the screen shows the count instead of a number, because a figure from two days and one from two hundred look identical and only one deserves action.
Claimed / AchievedThe rate the capacity sheet claims, against what declarations show the department really does. Reported, never applied — the software never silently corrects a master.
Plan allows / Actually tookPer article: the days the D-minus offsets allow end-to-end, against the real span from first declaration to last.
Window gone vs Work doneFor each open line: how much of its time has passed against how much of its work is complete. 65% of the window gone with 4% done is the picture of a line in trouble.
Reading / BecauseThe verdict band — on track, at risk, likely late, not started — and the reason in a sentence. Bands, not percentages: a percentage would be read as a probability, and it would be invented.
Money Cash out, week by week Set-up input
TermWhat it means
CommittedMoney the plan obliges you to spend in a given week — materials by their order-by dates, labour and packing by production dates.
Unit cost / Labour / Materials / Packing & freightThe cost build-up per article, as your accounts team enters it.
UnpricedWork in the plan with no cost entered — shown as a named gap, never estimated around.
By supplier / TermsThe same commitments grouped by who gets paid, with their payment terms.
What U&M provides
A cost per article (labour, material, packing), and supplier payment terms. One article's cost switches the screen on for that article.
Capacity sheet The 994 boxes everything is arithmetic on Set-up input

71 articles × 14 departments. Each box wants three numbers, and they come from PPC in an Excel workbook that loads back in one step.

TermWhat it means
Units per dayThe dedicated rate — see the everywhere-words. The most important number in the system, and the one most damaging to enter as a blended figure.
ManpowerThe crew size the rate was measured with. Without it, attendance cannot scale the day.
D-minus / Missing D-minusThe finish-by offset per article per department. A missing one blocks that article from being planned — deliberately, because guessing a deadline of zero would look normal and be wrong.
Articles routedHow many articles pass through each department — i.e. carry a rate there.
A department is due before something that feeds itThe contradiction check: an offset that finishes a department earlier than one feeding it. The current 71 findings trace to one route link awaiting your answer.
What U&M provides
The completed workbook from PPC — rate, crew and D-minus per box. The single biggest ask, and the single blocker: every date in the system is arithmetic on these numbers.
Masters What the factory is Set-up input
TermWhat it means
Product masterThe article list — code, name, category, and whether each can currently be planned (it needs a route and complete D-minus).
Route positionA department's display order on screens. The real sequencing is the dependency grid below, not this number.
Dependency gridThe what-feeds-what table: one tick per pair of departments. This is the route the scheduler walks, and the home of the machining / ply cutting question.
Yield %Per department: the share expected to pass. Drives the grossing-up of quantities.
Shifts / Overtime ceilingWhich shifts each department runs, and the maximum lawful overtime per person per day.
Machine master / Book downtimeEach department's machines, and planned out-of-service periods. A department's capacity scales with what is actually running. Empty until a machine list arrives.
HolidaysThe non-working calendar. Sundays are assumed; everything else is entered here and the schedule plans around it.
Bill of materialsPer article: the materials it consumes (feeds the Material screen).
Save everything to a fileA one-click export of masters, order book and production history — a copy U&M holds, independent of us and of the platform.
What U&M provides
Confirmation of the route (the dependency grid), the holiday calendar, a machine list per department, and parts lists — each its own small session, none daily.
Users Who can sign in, and as what Set-up input
TermWhat it means
RoleA job title carrying permissions: MD, planner, merchandiser, department head, HR, purchase, store, quality, maintenance, accounts, admin, kiosk. A person can hold several.
What each role can doThe permission list, readable on screen. Enforced in the database itself, so it holds no matter how the system is reached.
KioskThe one deliberately blind role: it can show the Floor display and nothing else — safe to leave signed in on a TV.
What U&M provides
Names and email addresses, a role for each, and one standing decision: should a department head see other departments' figures, or only their own?

The dependency, in one sentence each

The capacity sheet makes the plan real · orders give it something to plan · production and attendance, typed daily, let it compare promise with fact · parts lists and costs switch on the last two screens · and everything else on this list is the software answering back. Five set-up inputs, three daily habits, twelve screens of output.
