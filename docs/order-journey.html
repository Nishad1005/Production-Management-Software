In one minute

Yours

The article, the fourteen departments and the route are U&M’s own, as they are on your sheet. The order is made up for this document.

A stand-in

Every speed is invented. Nobody at U&M has given us a pieces-a-day figure yet, so every date here follows from a made-up one. The shape of the journey is real; the dates are not.

How to read it

One stop per screen, in the order they are used. The numbers on each picture are explained beneath it. The last box on every page says who does this at U&M, and when.

The whole journey in one sentence. Merchandising checks a date and enters the order; the software plans every department back from the container; stores buy what the plan says; each department enters two numbers a day and counts in what it receives; the plan re-learns every morning; and the order moves to Ready to stuff the moment the last department has finished. Nobody types a date after the first one.

Stop 1 The customer asks. Can we take it? Menu → Accept an order
  1. 1Pick the article. Betsy Counter Stool — Specter Pearl, from your own article list.
  2. 2The quantity: 240 pieces.
  3. 3The container date the customer wants. The software calls it the stuffing date: the day the container is loaded. Every other date is counted back from it.
  4. 4Press Check. The whole factory is planned again with this order in it, and then put back exactly as it was. Nothing is saved.
  5. 5The answer in one line: “11 of 14 steps cannot be made to this date.”
  6. 6Which steps, and why. Every department is listed with the day it would have to start and the day it must finish. The ones that cannot say what is wrong: Late means it would have had to start before today to make that date; Runway means the department before it finishes too late to leave it enough days.
  7. 7Take it anyway. The verdict is advice, not a lock. If the customer cannot wait, this carries the order to the book as it was checked and asks why you are taking it; the reason stays on the order, and the plan shows where it will break so the shortfall can be worked on from day one.
In the picture

23 Nov 2026 is too soon: 11 of the 14 steps cannot be done in the days they would have. Your sheet finishes the first step nearly three months before the container, so a container six weeks out means those steps should already have started.

Who does this, and when

Merchandising, while the customer is still on the phone. It takes a few seconds and changes nothing, so it can be tried with three dates in a row. Taking an order the plan cannot make is still U&M's call; the software records the call and shows the cost.

Stop 2 The date that can be promised Menu → Accept an order
  1. 1Only the date is changed, to 15 Feb 2027, and Check pressed again.
  2. 2The answer: “Every department can make this within its window.”
  3. 3Every row reads Clear, with the day each department would start. The first is Ply Cutting, on 21 Nov 2026.
In the picture

This is the date to promise. It was found in two tries and a few seconds, against everything already in the order book, and nothing was written down anywhere.

Who does this, and when

Merchandising, before answering the customer. If the customer cannot wait, the same screen with a smaller quantity, or What if with overtime in the department that fails, says what it would take.

Stop 3 The order is confirmed. Enter it once. Menu → Order book
  1. 1The order number from the ERP: SO/26-27/0501. When the ERP export arrives, this screen is filled from it instead of by hand.
  2. 2The customer, from the list.
  3. 3The article.
  4. 4The quantity. One order can ship in more than one container; each container is a line of its own, with its own date.
  5. 5The container date — the one the Check said yes to.
  6. 6Press Add order. The plan is worked out again straight away, with this order in it. The row below shows it in the book.
In the picture

The new row in the book: SO/26-27/0501, Nordic Living ApS, UT263 SPWL COU, 240 pieces, 1 container, stuffing 15 Feb 2027, confirmed. Every department's dates for this order exist from this moment.

Who does this, and when

Merchandising or PPC, the day the order is confirmed. Once, for the whole order.

Stop 4 The plan: every department, counted back from the container Menu → Schedule
  1. 1The order: SO/26-27/0501 line 1. The thin line across the top is its whole span.
  2. 2The first bar is Ply Cutting: 21 Nov 2026 to 24 Nov 2026, 279 pieces. Each bar is one department's work, from the day it starts to the day it must finish.
  3. 3The last bar is Final Packing, just before the container.
  4. 4The container day. Everything is counted back from here: your sheet says how many days before the container each department must be finished, and the software holds every department to it.
  5. 5A deadline mark. The thin black line on a bar is that department's own deadline from your sheet.
In the picture

14 departments, one after another, 68 working days from the first cut to the last carton. Green means the department has the days it needs. Nobody drew this: it follows from the sheet's day-counts, the quantity and each department's speed.

Who does this, and when

PPC reads it; nobody has to make it. A department head opens it to see when their part starts. A bar can be dragged to another day if there is a reason, and the software will then say what that costs.

Stop 5 What to buy, and by when Menu → Material
  1. 1Order now, or it will be late. Every material the plan needs that is not in the store, with the last day it can be ordered: the day the department needs it, less the supplier's lead time.
  2. 2One card per material. 5 are past the day they should have been ordered for the orders already running. The first is Leather, full grain: order by 17 Aug, 56 days from the supplier.
  3. 3Against the store. What the whole plan needs of each material, what was last counted, and whether that covers it.
  4. 4Leather is the long one: eight weeks from the supplier, so it is ordered before the first piece of wood is cut.
In the picture

The store screen does not know about one order; it knows about all of them together, which is the only way a shortage can be seen. A material that has never been counted says so, rather than pretending to be short or covered.

Who does this, and when

Stores count; purchase orders. Each morning, the “Order now” list is the shopping list.

Stop 6 The first day on the floor: Ply Cutting Menu → Production
  1. 1The department picks itself from the list. On a phone or the department's own computer, it stays picked.
  2. 2The day. It opens on today.
  3. 3Planned. 19 pieces of this order today — the day's share of the 279 this department must make.
  4. 4Good: how many came out right.
  5. 5Rejected: how many did not. Counted separately, never as a percentage.
  6. 6Not yet entered is what every job says until the figures are in.
  7. 7Entered. That is the whole entry: two numbers, once a day, per job.
In the picture

Between the day and the list, the screen also asks who came in, and the day's capacity follows from it. 19 asked, 19 made. Further down the route, Sanding will reject 4 pieces; they are entered the same way and appear on the Quality screen with the department's name on them.

Who does this, and when

Each department head, or whoever keeps the register, at the end of the shift. The plan does not move while people are working; the next morning's run takes what was made into account.

Stop 7 Handed over, and counted in Menu → Production
  1. 1Who sent it, and when. Machining declared these pieces; they now sit at Assembly's door.
  2. 2They made 65.
  3. 3You received. Enter what is actually there. Here 63.
  4. 4The difference is kept: “2 SHORT”. It is not smoothed over and it is not an argument; it is a record.
  5. 5Count in. From now on the software knows Assembly has the pieces, and what it owes the department after it.
In the picture

Two sides to every handover: the department that made it says so, the department that receives it counts it. Where both agree, nothing happens. Where they differ, the difference is on record with both names on it.

Who does this, and when

The receiving department, first thing in the shift, standing up, usually on a phone.

Stop 8 Where is it now? Menu → WIP
  1. 1How far through the route: 43%, 6 of 14 departments, container in 126d.
  2. 2The strip is the route, one block per department in order. Green is finished, amber is under way, grey has not started. The shape says where the work is faster than any number.
  3. 3Open it and every department is listed with what it has made against what it must make.
In the picture

Half the route is done. Everything on this screen is a count of pieces, declared by the department that made them and counted in by the next; no estimate anywhere. A department usually makes a piece or two more than the job needs, and that shows here too.

Who does this, and when

Anyone, any time. The MD's “where is the Nordic order?” is answered here without a phone call.

Stop 9 The same order, on the department’s wall Menu → Floor display
  1. 1Due today or overdue: what this department has to finish today, by order. Today: “Nothing due today. 9 jobs still ahead.” — our order is among the jobs still ahead.
  2. 2Waiting on: what is already due to this department from the ones feeding it and has not arrived, with what reached it so far.
In the picture

Stitching's screen, as a television on the wall would show it: no menu, no buttons, large type, and it remembers which department it is.

Who does this, and when

Nobody. It refreshes itself. One television per department is enough.

Stop 10 The plan learns from the floor Menu → Command centre → Schedule
  1. 1Made. After a run, a department that has declared the whole job no longer has a bar; it reads Made. 6 of 14 here.
  2. 2The rest is planned again from what remains: 8 bars still to come, each sized to what is still to make.
In the picture

Pressing Run the schedule on the Command centre takes a few seconds. A department that fell behind is not left in the past: its remaining work is moved to start from today, after the departments feeding it, and the reason reads Late if it still misses its date. Nothing that has been made is planned twice.

Who does this, and when

PPC, each morning, after attendance is entered and before the day starts. Any change to a master — a holiday, a machine down, a new order — runs it as well.

Stop 11 Ready to stuff Menu → WIP → Dashboard
  1. 1Ready to stuff. The order moves here the moment every department has declared the whole job: 100%, 14 of 14 departments.
  2. 2The same strip, all green.
  3. 3Containers ready on the MD's dashboard counts it: 1. Beside it, the nine figures the dashboard was asked for, all of them from the same entries the departments made along the way.
  4. 4Planned against made. Under the figures, every department's last fourteen working days as one row of squares: green made what was planned, amber most of it, red short, striped made with nothing planned. The days this order was entered are in it. Click a department and its fortnight is drawn as bars.
In the picture

From the customer's first question to this screen, nobody typed a date. The dates came from your sheet's day-counts and the speeds; the progress came from two numbers a day per department. The container date was kept.

Who does this, and when

Packing declares the last job; dispatch reads the list. What left the factory is the one thing not yet recorded, and is next.
