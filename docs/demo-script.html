Say this first, before any screen

“Everything you are about to see runs on your route — your 14 departments, your 71 articles. None of the numbers are yours. The rates, the days-before-shipment, the crew sizes and the 12 orders in it are ours, put there so the system could be used and argued with while we wait for PPC's sheet. There is a banner on every screen saying so, and it stays until your figures replace them.

So please tell me where the numbers are wrong. That is the useful part of today.”

The walkthrough

Command centre The whole factory in four numbers
On screen
12 shipment lines · 168 scheduled tasks · 18 breaches · 3 flagged days on the constraint. Below: Stitching marked as the constraint at 0.46 average and 2.00 peak, then a triaged list of flagged days.
“Four numbers describe the plan. A shipment line is the unit, not the order — one order going into two containers is two problems. A breach is work the plan says cannot be made in the time available. A flagged day is a department asked for more than it can do.

The panel on the right sorts those by how much time is left and labels each one with what is still possible at that notice. That is a label, not a recommendation — whether you actually run overtime depends on cash and on the customer, and the system does not know either.”
If they ask

Why is Stitching the constraint?

Because capacity above the bottleneck is decorative. It is the department with the highest average utilisation across the horizon, so it is where the next person or the next machine goes. It is ranked from Stitching, Stapling, Fitting — and it is our invented rates saying so, not yours.

How long does a re-plan take?

72 seconds on this data. It is a full re-plan of every line through every department, not a refresh. It will get faster.

Load heatmap The shape of the next six months
On screen
14 departments across 174 days. Green is at capacity, red is over. Click any cell for the orders and components on that day.
“Each cell is one department on one day, shaded by how much of the day the planned work consumes. A department can be making several things at once, so the figure is the sum of the fractions of the day each one takes — you cannot add units of legs to units of covers, but you can add the time they take. Anything over 1.00 is more work than there are hours.”
If they ask

Can we see what is causing a red day?

Click it. The panel underneath names the orders, the components and the quantity on that department on that day.

Schedule Where the dates come from, and moving one
On screen
A bar per line per department, scheduled backwards from each container stuffing date. Drag one to pin it.
“Nothing here was typed in. Every date is the stuffing date minus your D-minus offset, walked backwards through the route. Drag a bar and the plan re-flows around it, and the pin survives the next run — a planner's decision is not something the software gets to overwrite.

Every run writes a new version. Nothing is overwritten, so you can always put last week's plan next to what actually happened.”
Attention Everything the software has noticed, in one list
On screen
72 critical, 35 warnings, critical first and each row linking to the screen that clears it.
“Nothing on this screen is computed twice. Every line is a conclusion another screen already reached, put in one place and sorted by how soon it bites.

There is deliberately no way to dismiss one. An alert you can silence while it is still true stops being read within a week.”
If they ask

Why are there 71 route conflicts?

This is the question worth stopping on, and it is item 2 of the sheet you are leaving with them. One route link in the system says Machining feeds Ply Cutting; your D-minus offsets say the opposite. The software is reporting that the route and the offsets disagree, and it needs one of you to say which is right.

Accept an order Can we take this?
On screen
A quantity and a stuffing date in, a yes or no out — against the plan that already exists.
“This answers against a loaded factory, not an empty one. It is the difference between ‘we have the capacity’ and ‘we have the capacity once everything already promised is made’.”
What if Try a change without committing to it
On screen
A department, a date range and a factor. The result sits beside the live plan.
“Add people to Stitching for a fortnight and see what it does to every date in the factory, without touching the live plan. It writes a separate version that is never made current unless you promote it.”
Capacity sheet Where every number on every other screen came from
On screen
994 cells — 71 articles across 14 departments — every one of them filled in by us.
“This is the honest moment. Everything you have just seen is arithmetic on this sheet, and every figure in it is ours. Nothing on the previous six screens means anything about your factory until PPC replace them.

Loading your workbook replaces these cell by cell. Nothing else has to change, and our 12 orders come out in one command.”
If they ask

How long will filling this take?

994 cells sounds worse than it is — a rate that is the same across a family of articles can be filled once and copied down. A partly-filled sheet is genuinely useful and can come back more than once.

Forecast The screen that refuses to guess
On screen
Readiness first: 12 declarations across 6 days, and 0 of 12 rates measured. Below it, every figure reads too few to say.
“Finish here. This screen has a threshold of 10 observations, and under it, it shows you the count instead of a number.

A prediction from two days and a prediction from two hundred look identical on a screen, and only one of them should be acted on. Every other screen in this system is built the same way — when it does not know, it says so rather than filling the gap with something plausible.”
If they ask

When will it start predicting?

Once ten days of production have been declared against a rate. Today there are 6. It fills in on its own as the floor uses the system.

Close on the six asks

They are set out in full in DBBS/UM/KRAM/06 — hand that over rather than talking through it. Name them and stop:

  1. PPC's capacity workbook — 994 cells, three numbers each. Who, and by when?
  2. What feeds what — and today's version of it: does Machining feed Ply Cutting?
  3. One real export file out of Panipuri — and does it hold a stuffing date, or only a delivery date?
  4. A bill of materials and a cost, for one article to begin with.
  5. A floor plan.
  6. Names and emails, and who should see what.

Write down every figure anybody said was wrong, and who said it. That list is worth more than the workbook — it is what they will check first when the real data lands.
