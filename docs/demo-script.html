Demonstration script Thirty minutes through Kram, and the questions worth asking at the end of it

RefDBBS/UM/KRAM/03
ForU&M Designs
Runs onOffline draft
Length~30 min + questions

The order is the arc the software actually has: find a problem, understand it, try a fix, then show where the numbers come from and who keeps them up to date. Steps 5 and 9 are the two worth slowing down for.

Say this in the first minute, before anything else

The departments and the product codes are yours. Every capacity figure, every date and every order on the screen is made up. They are there so the screens have something to say. Getting your real numbers in is the next piece of work, and part of what today is for.

Worth being blunt about. The screen will show Ply Cutting making 120 a day, and if that goes unchallenged the whole session becomes an argument about a number nobody entered.

Before they arrive

  1. The demonstration runs on your laptop, not on the Netlify link. Open a terminal and start it:
    cd ~/Desktop/PPM
    npm run dev
    then open http://localhost:5173. Nothing goes over the network — the database is inside the browser — so nothing can fail live because of wifi.
  2. kraam.netlify.app is the other one. It is the hosted system, wired to the shared database, and it has no capacity figures in it yet — so its planning screens are empty by design. You use it once, at step 10, and its emptiness is the point of that step. Do not run the walkthrough on it.
  3. Sanity check. On localhost, the small table at the top right of the page should have a row reading Build — Offline draft. That is how the two tell each other apart at a glance.
  4. Command centre → Reset demo data. Not optional tidying. A browser that has opened Kram before keeps the database it built then, and the demonstration data has changed since — so the capacity sheet can come up completely empty. One click rebuilds it. Then Run the schedule once so the first screen is not blank.
  5. Have this script on a second screen, not on the one you are sharing.
  6. Know the one number. Stitching is the constraint at 38 a day. Almost every question about why something is flagged comes back to it.

The walkthrough

01What this is for1 min

Do

  1. Nothing. Do not share the screen yet.

Say

“Today you commit a stuffing date from experience — you know roughly what the factory can do. Kram checks that commitment against what each department can actually make, day by day, working backwards from the container. It finds the problem. It does not decide what to do about it.”

That last sentence matters. Everything in the software is built to report and stop there, and saying so early prevents “so it will tell us to do overtime?” half an hour in.

02Command centre — is the factory on track?4 min

Do

  1. Open Command centre.
  2. Click Run the schedule with Confirmed + probable selected.
  3. Point at the four figures.
  4. Point at Bottleneck utilisation.
  5. Point at Flag triage — read down the right column.

Say

The whole order book rescheduled in well under a second, and every run is saved as its own version — nothing is overwritten, so you can always go back to what you promised last week.

Breaches and flagged days are different things. A breach is work that cannot be made as planned. A flagged day is a department asked for more than it can produce.

“Stitching is the constraint. Capacity above the bottleneck is decorative — if stitching does 38 a day, the factory does 38 a day, whatever fitting can manage.”

Flag triage sorts by how much time is left and labels what is still possible at that lead time — hiring, then overtime and resequencing, then only a conversation with the customer. A label, not a recommendation.

03Load heatmap — which days hurt3 min

Do

  1. Open Load heatmap.
  2. Scroll sideways so they see the shape of it.
  3. Click a red cell.
  4. Point at an outlined (idle) cell nearby.

Say

Every department against every day. Red is over capacity. Clicking one shows exactly which orders are on that day and what share of the day each takes — so a flag is never just a colour.

“The empty ones matter as much as the red ones. Because it schedules backwards, work lands as late as it can — so you often get idle days sitting immediately before a department drowns. That is a resequencing opportunity you cannot see on a spreadsheet.”

04Schedule — the plan, and overriding it3 min

Do

  1. Open Schedule.
  2. Filter to Breaches only.
  3. Drag one bar a few days earlier.
  4. Type a reason. Save.
  5. Point at the pin appearing in the list below.

Say

One bar per task, per shipment line. The marker is that department's own deadline.

“A planner always knows something the system does not. So you can move anything — but it asks you why, and it makes you answer. A pin with no reason is indistinguishable from a mistake six weeks later.”

From then on the engine schedules around that pin and reports whatever it breaks, rather than quietly putting it back.

05Accept an order — the one that matters5 min

Do

  1. Open Accept an order.
  2. Pick Boden Dining Chair, quantity 600.
  3. Stuffing date about six weeks out. Click Check.
  4. Read the refusal and which departments break.
  5. Now push the date out by three weeks and check again.
  6. Go to Order book — show it is not there.

Say

“A customer is on the phone asking for 600 chairs in six weeks. This is the answer, before you give them one.”

It schedules the order provisionally against everything already committed, reports which departments break and by how much, then takes it out again. Nothing was added to the order book — check it yourself.

Moving the date and re-checking is the part to linger on. It turns “no” into “not that date, but this one”, which is a different conversation with a customer.

Everything else in the system finds problems after the commitment. This is the only screen that finds them before.

If it says yes first time, the book is quieter than expected on the day — put the quantity up to 800 and try again. The point of the step is watching a no become a yes, not the number.

06What if — trying it without risking it3 min

Do

  1. Open What if.
  2. Department Stitching, preset Department down.
  3. A window of about a fortnight. Run it.
  4. Click Compare.
  5. Do not promote it — say you could, and move on.

Say

A machine breaks, or you are deciding whether a second shift is worth it. This runs the whole plan again under that assumption and shows you the difference against the live one.

“The masters are never touched. The capacity change is applied for that one run and taken straight back out — so there is no way to experiment and accidentally leave the factory configured wrong.”

If a scenario turns out to be the right answer, one button makes it the live plan.

07Capacity sheet — where every number comes from4 min

Do

  1. Open Capacity sheet.
  2. Point at the four figures across the top.
  3. Switch Units per day → D-minus.
  4. Click Sanding on the first row, type 70, Enter.
  5. Wait for the warning panel. Read it out.
  6. Put it back to 56. Watch it clear.

Say

“This is your capacity spreadsheet, in the software. Same grid, except the engine reads it. Six articles here; you have seventy-one.”

A units figure means the article passes through that department at that rate. A blank means it does not go there at all. The rate is what that department makes in a day doing nothing else — that convention is what lets the software add up a mixed day correctly, and it is the number we need from PPC.

Then the guard: setting Sanding to finish before Assembly, which feeds it, is a contradiction. It names both departments and says whether it is already causing false warnings. It does not correct anything — which of the two figures is wrong is your call, not the software's.

08Masters — what feeds what3 min

Do

  1. Open Masters, scroll to What feeds what.
  2. Point at the four Entry point tags.
  3. Trace one row aloud: Stapling waits for Foam Pasting and Stitching.

Say

This is the structure PPC confirmed. Four departments start on their own; three streams — frame, fabric and metal — come together late.

“This drives two things. A department is held back until everything feeding it is due, so wire a feeder into the wrong place and you get warnings that are not real. And losses compound along these lines only — so a leg is never made in larger numbers to cover stitching's rejects, because a leg never goes near stitching.”

Editable here at any time, and the schedule recalculates as soon as you change it.

09Production — the daily sheet, replaced4 min

Do

  1. Open Production.
  2. Department ASSY — Assembly.
  3. Point at Handed to you, not yet counted in.
  4. Type a smaller number than declared. Watch the short tag appear. Click Count in.
  5. Switch to STITCH and pick a date from the offered days.
  6. Enter a good figure and a rejected figure. Enter.

Say

“This replaces the daily production sheet. Two jobs, in the order you actually do them: count in what arrived from the bench before you, then write down what you made.”

The department before you says what it made; you count what turned up. If they disagree, the difference is kept, not smoothed over. That gap is the reason for counting it twice.

Good and rejected go in separately because both are numbers a supervisor can stand behind. The percentage is worked out from them, and gets compared against the yield on Masters — which is how an assumption slowly turns into a measurement.

Entering output moves no dates. The plan stays where it is, because it is what everyone is working to today.

10It is real, and it is shared2 min

Do

  1. Open kraam.netlify.app in a second tab.
  2. Sign in.
  3. Open Users, point at the roles.

Say

Everything so far ran entirely inside the browser, which is why it is instant and needs nothing installed. This is the same software against the shared database — one plan, everyone looking at it.

“Who can see and change what is enforced in the database, not in the screen. Hiding a menu is a convenience; being unable to read the data is the actual rule.”

It is deliberately empty of capacity figures. That is the ask, and it leads straight into the questions.

Say what is not there, before they find it

  • The MD dashboard from the deck is not built. Six of its nine figures — OTIF, daily production, WIP value, efficiency, rejections, shortages — need actuals from the floor. Those only start existing now that Production is in. Building it earlier would have meant six invented numbers sitting beside three real ones.
  • WIP is counted, not valued. Rupee value needs a component cost master we have not loaded.
  • Panipuri orders are not imported yet. Panipuri gives files, not an interface, so it will be a file drop — and the shape of that file is a question for whoever exports it.

The questions — this is the point of the session

Is the capacity sheet the right shape?

It is modelled on the one you sent. What is missing from it, and what on it do you not actually use?

Can you give a dedicated-day rate?

“What this department makes in a day doing only this article.” If you only ever think in mixed days, we need to know now — it changes how the arithmetic works.

Who enters production, and when?

End of shift, or as it happens? On a phone on the floor, or on a computer in an office? It changes what that screen should be.

Will the receiving department really count?

Two-sided handovers are the honest model, but they are twice the typing. Is that a step too far for your floor?

What would you open first thing?

If you looked at one screen at 9am, which is it — and what would you want on it that is not there?

What would stop you using this?

The most useful answer of the session, and the one people only give if you ask for it directly.

Afterwards

  1. If anything real was entered during the session — Masters → Save masters to a file. The offline build lives in one browser; the file is how that work travels.
  2. The blocking item is the capacity sheet. Nothing on the hosted system means anything until PPC's rates and D-minus figures are in it. Everything else can wait; this cannot.
  3. Write down what they said in their words, the same day. The phrasing is worth as much as the content.
