Thirty minutes before they arrive

  1. Press Run the schedule on the Command centre (Confirmed + probable) and let its 70 seconds happen with nobody watching. Do not press it twice, and do not open a second tab while it runs.
  2. Regenerate this script so its numbers match the fresh plan: npm run demo:script, then npm run pdf docs/demo-script.html.
  3. Click through Command centre → Load heatmap → Attention → Forecast once, so the first click in the room is instant.
  4. Decide the route question. 71 of the red items trace back to one route question — decide before the meeting whether to leave it in and open with it, or untick it (Masters → dependency grid) and re-run.
  5. Have the blank capacity workbook and the printed KRAM/06 sheet ready to hand over.

If anything shows a red banner during the demo: read it aloud calmly, press the × on it, and carry on. This software's whole philosophy is that it fails out loud instead of pretending — that is a feature you can point at, but only if you do not flinch.

“Everything you are about to see runs on your factory's shape — your 14 departments, your 70 products, the order your work actually flows in. None of the numbers are yours yet. The speeds, the day-counts, the crew sizes and the 12 orders in it are ours, invented so you could see the software working while we wait for your planning team's figures. So when a figure looks wrong to you — and some will — say so. That is the most useful thing that can happen in this room today.”

Part 1

The promise, and whether it can be kept

Four screens that show the whole factory at once. This is the half that must land — everything after it is supporting detail.

Command centre The whole factory in four numbers ~4 min
On screen
12 shipment lines · 168 scheduled jobs · 18 that cannot be done in the time available · Stitching named as the tightest department. On the right, a short list of the days that are overloaded, each labelled with what can still be done about it.
“This is the whole factory on one page. Along the top: how many container loads you have promised, how many jobs that breaks into across your departments, and — in red — how many of those jobs do not fit in the time available.

Below, the system names your tightest department. That is where an extra pair of hands or an extra machine helps the whole factory, rather than just one room.

And on the right, the days that are overloaded — sorted by how much warning you still have. Something three months away says overtime or subcontract would fix this. Something next week says this needs a phone call to the customer. The label tells you what is still physically possible; the decision stays with you.

Remember these two numbers — 12 shipments, 168 jobs. In a few minutes we are going to take a new order in front of you and you will watch both of them move.”
If they ask

Who decided those labels?

Arithmetic, not opinion. With 45 days of notice you can hire; with 15 you can run overtime; with less, the only honest option left involves the customer. The thresholds are written down and can be changed.

How fresh is this?

It is a snapshot, replanned on demand. Pressing the dark button redoes the whole plan from scratch — it takes about 70 seconds because it genuinely recalculates every job in the factory, and every version is kept, so last month's plan can always be pulled back and compared with what actually happened.

Load heatmap Six months of work, coloured in ~3 min
On screen
A grid: 14 departments down the side, 174 days across. Light green is partly busy, dark green is full, red is overloaded.
DoScroll sideways slowly, then click one red cell and let the panel underneath open.
“Every square is one department on one day. The darker it is, the fuller that day already is. Red means the day holds more work than there are hours.

A department often makes several different things at once, so the software adds up the share of the day each job takes rather than the pieces — you cannot add chairs to cushions, but you can add the hours they take. When those shares add up past a full day, the square goes red.

Click a red square and it tells you exactly which orders are on top of each other that day.”
Schedule Where every date comes from ~3 min
On screen
A horizontal bar for each order at each department, laid out on a calendar.
DoPoint at one order's bars and trace them right to left, ending at the ship date. If you drag a bar, that is fine — it goes into the practice data and is removed with it.
“Nobody typed any of these dates in. The software starts from the day the container must be stuffed and works backwards: packing must finish three days before the container, final checking a week before, stitching twenty-six days before, and so on — using the day-counts your own planning team will give us for each product.

If a planner knows better — a buyer visit, a material delay — they drag the bar. The plan reshuffles itself around that decision and remembers it. The software never quietly undoes what a person decided.”
If they ask

Can we push an order to a different date?

Two honest ways. To move one department's work a few days, drag its bar and pin it — the plan reshuffles around your decision. To move the whole container, change the shipment line's date on the Order book — remove the line, add it back with the new date — and re-plan. Deliberately two different acts, because they are two different promises: one is internal sequencing, the other changes what the customer was told.

Attention The to-do list the software writes for itself ~4 min
On screen
71 red items and 35 amber ones, most urgent first. Each one is a sentence, and clicking it opens the screen where it can be fixed.
“Everything the software has noticed, in one list, in plain sentences — this order cannot be made in time, this department is overloaded on this date, this material should have been ordered by now. The red ones need an answer today.

Two things are deliberate here. Every line takes you straight to the screen that fixes it. And there is no way to dismiss one — an alert you can silence while it is still true becomes wallpaper within a week, and then nobody reads any of them.”
If they ask

Why are 70 of them the same complaint?

Because the software has caught a genuine contradiction, and it is the first question we have for your team today: our records show machining feeding ply cutting, and the day-counts say the opposite. One of them is wrong. Whoever runs production can settle it in a sentence, and 70 red items disappear — this is the software doing exactly its job.

The life of one order

Where the numbers live, and the close

Capacity sheet The sheet everything else is arithmetic on ~3 min
On screen
980 boxes — 70 products across 14 departments — every one filled in by us, and labelled as such.
“This is the honest moment of the day. Every screen you have seen is arithmetic on this sheet: how many of each product each department can make in a day, and how many days before the container each must finish. Every number in it is ours, not yours. Your planning team gets this as an ordinary Excel sheet in a layout they already use. When it comes back, it replaces our numbers box by box — a partly-filled sheet is genuinely useful, and it can come back more than once.”
If they ask

980 boxes — how long will that take?

Less than it sounds. A rate that is the same across a whole family of products is filled once and copied down. And the day-count column is one number per department for most products. The session with your planning team is half a day, not a week.

Forecast The screen that refuses to guess ~3 min
On screen
Its first panel: 12 production entries across 6 days so far. Below, nearly every figure reads too few to say.
“I want to finish here, on the screen that mostly declines to answer.

In time this page will tell you what each department really achieves per day, how long each product really takes end to end, and which shipments look like missing their container. But it has a rule: under ten days of real history it says too few to say and shows you the count instead of a number — because a prediction from two days and one from two hundred look identical on a screen, and only one of them deserves to be acted on.

Every screen you have seen today is built on that same principle. Where this software does not know, it says so — it never fills the gap with something plausible. That is the property you are actually buying.”
If they ask

When does it start answering?

On its own, about ten working days after your floor starts writing down production. There are 6 days of practice entries in it now. Nothing to configure — it earns each number as the history arrives.

Close on the six asks

They are set out in full in DBBS/UM/KRAM/06 — hand the sheet over rather than talking through it. Name them and stop:

  1. The capacity workbook — 980 boxes, in Excel, from your planning team. Who, and by when?
  2. What feeds what — starting with today's question: does machining feed ply cutting?
  3. One real export file out of Panipuri — and does it hold a container stuffing date, or only a delivery date?
  4. A parts list and a cost, for one product to begin with.
  5. A floor plan.
  6. Names and emails, and who should see what.

Write down every figure anybody said was wrong, and who said it. That list is worth more than the workbook — it is what they will check first when the real data lands.
