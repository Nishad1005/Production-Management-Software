Demonstration script Thirty minutes through Kram, and the questions worth asking at the end of it

RefDBBS/UM/KRAM/03
ForU&M Designs
Runs onOffline draft
Length~45 min, or 20 short

Twenty screens, in the order the software actually tells its story: let it tell you what is wrong, follow one order through the factory, then show where the numbers come from and who keeps them up to date. If you have twenty minutes rather than forty-five, the steps marked core are the walkthrough and the rest are answers to questions they will ask anyway.

Say this in the first minute, before anything else

“The departments, the product codes and the cost structure are yours. Every capacity figure, every date, every order and every rupee on the screen is made up. They are there so the screens have something to say. Getting your real numbers in is the next piece of work, and part of what today is for.”

Worth being blunt about. The screen will show Ply Cutting making 120 a day, and if that goes unchallenged the whole session becomes an argument about a number nobody entered. The one exception is worth naming too: the cost breakdown on the Betsy chair is their own costing sheet, line for line, and saying so buys credit for everything else.

Before they arrive

  1. The demonstration runs on your laptop, not on the Netlify link. cd ~/Desktop/PPM then npm run dev, and open http://localhost:5173. Nothing goes over the network — the database is inside the browser — so nothing can fail live because of wifi.
  2. Sanity check. The table at the top right should read Build — Offline draft. That is how the offline demonstration and the hosted system tell each other apart at a glance.
  3. Command centre → Reset demo data, then Run the schedule once. Not optional tidying: a browser that has opened Kram before keeps the database it built then, and the demonstration data has changed a great deal since.
  4. Know the one number. Stitching is the constraint. Almost every question about why something is flagged comes back to it.
  5. Have this script on a second screen, not the one you are sharing.
01What this is for1 min · core

Do

  1. Nothing. Do not share the screen yet.

Say

“Today you commit a stuffing date from experience — you know roughly what the factory can do. Kram checks that commitment against what each department can actually make, day by day, working backwards from the container. It finds the problem. It does not decide what to do about it.”

That last sentence prevents “so it will tell us to do overtime?” half an hour in. Everything in the software reports and stops.

02Attention — the software tells you4 min · core

Do

  1. Open on Attention. Do not start on the command centre.
  2. Read the top two or three criticals aloud, whatever they are today.
  3. Click Go to the screen that fixes this on one of them, then come back.
  4. Point at the red count in the header — it is on every screen.

Say

“Your own brief asks for alerts so you respond in time rather than in a crisis. This is that. Nothing here is a new calculation — every line is something one of the other screens already worked out. You do not have to go looking.”

This is deck slide 2, objective five, and opening on it reframes the whole demonstration: the software is something that tells you, not something you consult. If they ask why there is no dismiss button: an alert you can silence while it is still true stops being read.

03Command centre — is the factory on track?4 min · core

Do

  1. Run the schedule with confirmed only, then again including probable. Watch the figures move.
  2. Read the four figures: lines, tasks, breaches, flagged days.
  3. Scroll to bottleneck utilisation and name the constraint.
  4. Show flag triage and its three lead-time bands.

Say

“A breach is work that cannot be made as planned. A flagged day is a department asked for more than it can produce. They are different things and both matter. Capacity above the bottleneck is decorative — if stitching runs at 38 a day, the factory runs at 38.”

Triage is a sort and a label, never a recommendation: over 45 days you can hire, 15 to 45 you can work overtime or resequence, under 15 the honest answer is a conversation with the customer.

04Factory map — the correction they gave us3 min

Do

  1. Open Factory map. Let them look before you talk.
  2. Trace the three streams: frame, fabric, metal — and where they join.
  3. Say plainly that it is a flow map, not a floor plan.

Say

“When we showed you the route as a single line, you told us most of these run alongside each other. You were right and we rebuilt it. Columns here run in parallel — nothing in a column waits for anything else in it.”

Credit where it is theirs. This is also the moment to ask for a floor plan: a real layout can be laid under this drawing, and we have deliberately not invented one.

05Heatmap and schedule — where it hurts, and moving it5 min · core

Do

  1. Load heatmap: point at a red block and click it.
  2. Schedule: filter to one department, then drag a bar two days later.
  3. Give a reason when asked, then re-run the schedule and show the pin held.

Say

“Utilisation adds up across everything a department is making that day. It is a share of the day, not a count of chairs — which is why legs and covers can be added together at all.”

The pin is the point: the planner overrules the engine, gives a reason, and every later run honours it. Kram never silently un-decides something a person decided.

06Accept an order — before the commitment4 min · core

Do

  1. Accept an order: 5,000 units, four weeks out.
  2. Let it refuse. Read which department cannot do it and by how much.
  3. Try again with a date six months out and watch it accept.

Say

“Every other screen finds a problem after you have committed. This one finds it before. A merchandiser can answer a customer in the time it takes to type the quantity.”

If one thing lands today, make it this. It is the only screen that changes a decision rather than reporting on one.

07What if — try it before you do it3 min

Do

  1. Add a shift to stitching, or move a stuffing date, and run the scenario.
  2. Show the comparison against the live plan, then promote it.

Say

“A scenario is a full schedule run that is not live. You compare it side by side, and it only becomes the plan when somebody says so.”

08My department, and Production on a phone5 min · core

Do

  1. My department → Stapling. Read “holding you up” and “what you owe”.
  2. Production: count in a handover, then declare a day's output.
  3. If you can, do this bit on your phone with the screen mirrored.

Say

“You asked for this in your own words: what is pending for the day, in shipping-date order, and which department a component has to come from so I can start. The handover is counted twice on purpose — the bench that made it and the bench that received it — and the difference is kept rather than smoothed away.”

The phone matters: they told us entry happens on the floor as it happens. Every control on these screens is thumb-sized, and the browser check enforces it.

09WIP — where everything is3 min · core

Do

  1. WIP → open a line in progress and expand it.
  2. Read it department by department: asked for, declared, counted in.

Say

“Where is order SO-1234 is answered as which bench it is sitting at, not as a percentage. Counted, not valued — the rupee figure is a separate question and it needs a cost.”

10Manpower — the first thing on your follow-up list4 min

Do

  1. Manpower → mark somebody out under Deployment.
  2. Go to Production → Today's capacity for that department and show the figure has moved.
  3. Come back and read “where overtime would close the gap”.

Say

“Slide 18 of your own deck asks HR for manpower and overtime tracking. Marking one person out changes what that department can make today, and the plan re-runs. The overtime arithmetic is your capacity model's, not ours — the tests check us against it.”

Days already past are not offered for overtime. Nobody can staff yesterday, and offering to would be advice that cannot be taken.

11Material — the ordering date4 min

Do

  1. Material → read the top of “order now, or it will be late”.
  2. Scroll to “against the store” and point at the three states.
  3. Type a count into On hand for a never-counted material.

Say

“Also slide 18: material ordering date to the supplier, D-minus article-wise. Counted back from the day the department that uses it starts — leather when cutting begins, not when the container sails — less the supplier's lead time.”

The third state is the one to dwell on. Covered, short, and nobody has counted it — an uncounted shelf is not an empty one, and calling it a shortage would bury the real ones.

12Quality — which two causes fix half of it3 min

Do

  1. Quality → read the top two bars and the running percentage.
  2. Point at the amber “not attributed to a cause” bar.
  3. Show a department running below the yield its master claims.

Say

“A rejection rate tells a department it is bad at something without saying what. Causes are grouped by who can fix them — workmanship to the department, material to purchase, machine to maintenance.”

Nobody is required to explain every reject. The unexplained balance is shown at its real size, because an unexplained majority makes the rest of the screen a guess.

13Money — their costing sheet, as data4 min

Do

  1. Money → open the Betsy chair's cost breakdown.
  2. Let them recognise it. Twenty-six lines, ₹16,759.71.
  3. Scroll up to money out by week and read the unpriced caveat.

Say

“This is your costing sheet, line for line — wood, plywood, foam, leather, packing, labour, finishing, CNF. It is the one screen today where the numbers are genuinely yours.”

Then the limit, in the same breath: Kram knows costs and not prices. No order carries a value, so it can say what the factory spends and never what it earns. Do not let them leave thinking it does cash flow both ways.

14Forecast — the screen that refuses3 min

Do

  1. Forecast → read the readiness panel first.
  2. Point at a rate saying too few to say.
  3. Show the shipment risk bands and the “because” column.

Say

“Under ten observations it will not state a figure — it shows the count instead. A number from two days and a number from two hundred look identical on a screen, and only one of them should be acted on.”

Expect “so it does not work yet”. The answer: it works exactly as it should on a factory with no history. On the hosted system today every panel here would say the same thing, and in three months it will not. Risk is banded rather than scored for the same reason — a percentage would be read as a probability we have not earned.

15Dashboard — and the number it refuses to invent3 min · core

Do

  1. Dashboard → the ten KPI cards.
  2. Stop on WIP value showing a dash, and read what it says.
  3. Scroll to “planned against made”.

Say

“Nine of these are your slide 6. The tenth is WIP in units, which needs no cost data. WIP value is empty because nobody has costed the article that is on the floor — and it says so instead of showing a zero.”

This is the single best thing to show an MD. A dashboard is believed; one that refuses to invent a figure is worth more than one that fills every box.

16The floor display2 min

Do

  1. Open #/display in a second tab, full screen.
  2. Pick a department and stand back from the laptop.

Say

“This is the LED screen from your slide 3. One department, what is due today, what it is waiting on, who is in. No navigation, and it refreshes itself — you put it on a wall and nobody touches it.”

17Where the numbers come from5 min · core

Do

  1. Capacity sheet → type a rate into a blank cell and watch the count change.
  2. Switch to D-minus and enter a contradiction; let the warning appear.
  3. Masters → “What feeds what”, then the Machines panel.
  4. Book downtime on a machine and show the department's day drop.

Say

“Everything you have seen comes from this grid. Seventy-one of your articles against fourteen departments, and today not one figure in it is real. This is the piece of work that turns the demonstration into a plan.”

The contradiction warning is worth provoking: it names both departments and refuses to guess which figure is wrong. That is the software's whole character in one interaction.

18The hosted system, and why it is empty3 min · core

Do

  1. Open kraam.netlify.app and sign in.
  2. Open the capacity sheet. Let the emptiness sit there.
  3. Show Users and the twelve roles.

Say

“Same software, shared database, real accounts. Your fourteen departments and seventy-one SKUs are already in it. What is missing is 994 numbers, and they are the ones only PPC can give us.”

Ending on the empty grid is deliberate. It makes the ask concrete and it is the truth: everything else is built.

What to ask them, while you have them

Can PPC fill in the workbook?

The sheet we send is laid out exactly as their capacity sheet already is. Partly filled is genuinely useful; it can come back more than once.

Is the what-feeds-what table right?

Still owed from our note of 12 August. Everything else is read through it, so a wrong edge quietly poisons every warning.

One export file out of Panipuri

Untidied. And the question that decides the workflow: does Panipuri hold a container stuffing date at all, or only a customer delivery date?

Who should see what?

Every role currently reads everything. Should maintenance see the customer order book? Easier decided now than after twelve people have logins.

A floor plan

The factory map draws the route because nobody has given us a layout. A real plan can be laid under it.

Bills of materials, and costs

How much of each material per chair, and at which department. The screens are waiting; the data is not there.

Afterwards, the same day

  1. If anything real was entered during the session — Masters → Save masters to a file, and Save everything to a file if any production was declared. The offline build lives in one browser.
  2. The blocking item is the capacity sheet. Nothing on the hosted system means anything until PPC's rates and D-minus figures are in it. Everything else can wait; this cannot.
  3. Write down what they said in their words, the same day. The phrasing is worth as much as the content — “on a phone on the floor as it happens” shaped three screens.
