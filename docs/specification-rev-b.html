01

Product summary

Kram (क्रम — sequence) schedules every open order backwards from its container stuffing date, spreads the work across the production route by component, and flags the days on which a department is asked to produce more than it can.

It then records what actually happened. Heads of department enter completed component counts, the next department accepts them, and the gap between plan and actual feeds back into the capacity figures the engine uses. The system becomes more accurate the longer it runs.

What Kram is not

It does not decide. It reports load against capacity, shortfall in hours and people, and the slack available in the same window. Whether to run overtime, add people, resequence or talk to the customer remains a production decision resting on material, cash and customer relationships the system cannot see.

Why multi-shift was the serious one

A department running two shifts has roughly double the daily capacity, the overtime ceiling applies per person per shift rather than per day, and attendance is recorded per shift. With a single headcount field, every capacity figure in the system would have been wrong — and wrong in a way that looks entirely normal on screen.

04

Core concepts

The shipment line is the scheduling unit

Orders ship in phases, so an order is not a single dated commitment. A 1,000-chair order may leave as 400 on 1 August and 600 on 20 August — two independent backward schedules from two stuffing dates. Every date calculation keys on the shipment line, never the order.

D-minus is an article × department matrix

Each article takes a different time through each department, so the offset is held per pair and entered manually. Adding a department creates blank rows flagged incomplete rather than defaulting to zero, so a missing value is visible rather than silently producing an impossible schedule.

Departments produce components, not chairs

chairs_ready = MIN over components of ( accepted_qty / bom_qty_per_unit )

  400 legs / 4        = 100
   80 seat frames / 1 =  80   ← limiting
  100 back frames / 1 = 100
  chairs_ready = 80   (100 legs and 20 back frames stranded)

A department can therefore be ahead and behind at the same time. Stranded components are real material and real labour producing nothing shippable, and must be surfaced or they accumulate invisibly.

Capacity is per department, per shift, per component

Rev B's central correction. A single "40 a day" figure conceals three separate variables, each of which moves independently: which shifts are running, how many people are on them, and which component is being made.

Yield compounds backwards

Losses multiply along the route, so a department must produce the shipped quantity divided by its own yield and the yield of every department after it. Five departments at 98% each cost roughly a tenth of factory capacity.

Pins are respected, not overwritten

A planner who drags a task has made a decision the engine cannot see the reasons for. Every subsequent run honours active pins and schedules around them, and reports any breach a pin causes rather than quietly undoing it. A schedule that silently reverts a planner's work will not be used twice.

Why not per-order labour costing

A department runs several orders in a shift. Splitting its hours between them requires every worker to log which order they touched — a discipline shop floors reliably fail to sustain, and the point at which systems like this are abandoned. Department-level costing gives most of the value for a fraction of the friction, and it is the figure that feeds the planner. Per-order costing can be added later if the floor proves it will maintain the data.

11

Scheduling engine

A pure function of the masters and the order book. It writes a new schedule_run and never mutates an existing one, so any past plan can be recovered and compared against what actually happened.

for each shipment_line where confidence in (filter)
  for each department in route (by route_position)
    dminus   = article_dept_dminus[article, department]
    due_date = roll_back_to_working_day( stuffing_date - dminus )

    for each component in article_bom[article] made by department
      gross = line.qty x bom.qty_per_unit
            / product( yield of this and all downstream departments )

      capacity = SUM over active shifts of
                 ( capacity_override(dept, shift, component, date)
                   ?? component_rates[component, dept, shift] )

      days   = ceil( gross / capacity )
      window = last `days` working days ending at due_date

      if pin exists          → honour pinned_start_date, re-derive window
      if window.start < material_ready_date  → breach: material
      if window.start < upstream_due_date    → breach: runway

      distribute capacity per day, remainder on the earliest day
      accumulate into schedule_daily_load

Feasibility checks

  • Runway breach. Working days between consecutive D-minus dates are fewer than the downstream department needs. Overtime cannot fix this — the department physically cannot start earlier under batch handoff.
  • Material breach. The window opens before material is available. The schedule is arithmetically valid and physically impossible.
  • Pin breach. A manual pin has pushed a task past its due date. Reported, not corrected.

Scale

324 orders averaging two shipment lines, across seven departments, three components and up to three shifts gives roughly 40,000 tasks and 300,000–400,000 daily-load rows per run. A set-based Postgres workload measured in seconds. Recomputation runs on order or master change (debounced), on demand, and nightly. What-if runs write a non-current run for side-by-side comparison, then discard or promote.

WIP mechanics

Two-sided handoff

EventMovementEffect
Wood finishes 100 legsdeclared_outLeaves wood's in-hand, enters the buffer
Sanding accepts 95accepted_inEnters sanding's in-hand
5 unreconciled—Flagged for same-day resolution
Sanding rejects 3rejected + returnedReturns to wood with a reason code
Wood scraps 1scrappedPermanently removed; increases remaining requirement
in_hand      = accepted_in - declared_out - scrapped + returned_in
chairs_ready = MIN over components of ( in_hand / bom.qty_per_unit )
stranded     = in_hand - ( chairs_ready x bom.qty_per_unit )

The acknowledgement step is not optional

A single-entry system drifts. Wood says 100, sanding says 95, and within a month no figure is trusted. Two-sided entry with same-day flagging is the difference between a WIP ledger people rely on and a screen they stop opening.

Department screen

Kiosk view on a wall-mounted tablet or LED panel per department, auto-refreshing, plus an entry mode for the HOD. Because the engine already knows each department's D-minus date, the screen shows commitments rather than raw counts.

  • Ready for me — declared out upstream, not yet accepted
  • In hand — accepted, not yet declared out, stranded flagged
  • Due — order, article, component, remaining, days to D-minus
  • Entry — scan or select, count, submit

Entry must complete in under about fifteen seconds or it will not happen consistently, and inconsistent data is worse than none. Barcode or QR scanning of the order and component exists for exactly this reason; every other design decision on this screen is subordinate to it.

Manpower & overtime

units_per_person_hour = shift_capacity / ( shift_headcount x net_hours )

shortfall_hours = shortfall_units / ( units_per_person_hour x ot_efficiency )
ot_hours_person = shortfall_hours / shift_headcount

if ot_hours_person <= shift.max_ot_hours   → within overtime
else
  covered = shift_headcount x max_ot_hours x units_per_person_hour x ot_efficiency
  extra   = ceil( (shortfall_units - covered) / (units_per_person_hour x net_hours) )

Overtime efficiency defaults to 85%. Hour eleven does not produce what hour three produces, and planning overtime at full productivity is the standard way these systems overpromise. It is configurable so the assumption stays visible.

Extra people are counted at full productivity, which is generous — a new hire is not at standard output in week one. Reported headcount shortfalls should be read as a floor.

Skill mix modifies effective capacity: a shift staffed with a higher proportion of unskilled workers produces less than its sanctioned rate. The weighting factor per skill level is configurable and defaults to parity until measured.

Self-correction

Actual attendance and actual output together give real productivity per department per shift per day, written back as the measured capacity figure with is_measured set. This is what makes the engine improve: capacity stops being an assertion and becomes a measurement.

Planning outputs

Four views over the same schedule run. Three of these were proposed during design and omitted from Rev A.

Load heatmap

Department × date grid, coloured by load against capacity. The daily symptom view.

Bottleneck utilisation New

Average utilisation per department across the horizon. The heatmap shows which days hurt; this shows which department is structurally the constraint. Capacity above the bottleneck is decorative — if stitching runs at 30 a day against wood's 40, the factory's real throughput is 30. This is the view that answers where to put the next person or the next machine, and the heatmap cannot.

Flag triage New

HorizonStill possible
45+ daysHiring
15–45 daysOvertime, resequencing, subcontract
Under 15 daysCustomer conversation

A sort and a label, no recommendation attached. It converts a flat list of two hundred flags into a triaged one, which is the difference between a report people act on and one they scroll past.

Idle capacity New

Backward scheduling places work as late as possible, so empty days frequently sit immediately before a breach. Reporting idle days alongside flagged days is half the value of the exercise — and the trend matters more than the number, because a floor with no idle days has no absorption left for the next rush order.

Gantt with manual pins

Drag-and-drop rescheduling writing to schedule_pins, with a mandatory reason. Pins survive every subsequent run. Given 324 orders, the Gantt is filtered by department, customer or date window rather than attempting to render the whole book.

Order acceptance check

A merchandiser enters a proposed article, quantity and stuffing date. Kram provisionally schedules it against the live book and reports which departments breach and when, or returns the earliest date that breaches nothing. Everything else finds problems after the commitment; this finds them before, which makes it the highest-value screen in the product.

ERP import

Panipuri exports files. No API, no database access — so the bridge is an import module with a validation gate. Nothing lands directly in live tables.

FeedContentsFrequency
sales_ordersCustomer PO, article, quantity, datesDaily
article_masterArticle codes and descriptionsOn change

purchase_ordersOpen POs, supplier, expected dateDaily
grnReceipts against PODaily

  • Saved column mappings. An admin maps export columns to Kram fields once per feed. When the export format changes — and it will — someone remaps on a screen instead of waiting for a release.
  • Idempotent on the ERP document number. Re-uploading the same file updates rather than duplicating. Without it, one double-click creates hundreds of phantom orders.
  • Row-level validation with preview. Errors flagged per row, batch committed only after a person confirms. Partial commits allowed; rejected rows downloadable for correction.

Data freshness must be shown honestly

The concept deck carries a single "Live" badge. That is only true of data Kram captures itself. Production and manpower are near real-time because HODs and HR enter them directly; orders, material, purchase orders and stock are only as current as the last export. Each panel therefore carries its own timestamp rather than one global badge. An MD acting on a stale number because the screen claimed to be live is the fastest way to lose confidence in the system.

AI position

Most of what the concept deck describes as AI is deterministic logic. That is good news, and should be said plainly to the client rather than sold as something else.

ClaimWhat it actually isPhase
Identifying likely bottlenecksCapacity flagging — arithmetic2
Predicting shipment riskRemaining work vs days vs capacity — arithmetic2
What-if simulationRe-running the engine on altered inputs2
Optimal production sequenceJob-shop scheduling — classical optimisation10
—Natural-language query and daily narrative — LLM9
—Cycle-time, lead-time, rejection prediction — ML10

Deterministic logic is the right answer for the first three, and not as a compromise. A production head can see exactly why a flag fired and will act on it; a model that says "this order is at risk" without showing its reasoning gets ignored by the third week.

Predictive models cannot come first

Cycle-time prediction, supplier lead-time forecasting and rejection likelihood all need historical actuals that do not exist yet. The WIP tracker must run for roughly six months before there is anything to train on. This should be put to the client in writing now, because it protects the engagement from a commitment that cannot be met on any timeline.

L

Phase 2 is the one to validate hardest

Every later phase inherits its date arithmetic. If the working-day counting is out by one, all eleven phases are wrong and nothing on screen will look unusual. Reproducing the windows of two or three completed orders is what proves it.

A usable product exists at Phase 4

Phases 0 to 4 deliver the planner, the flagger, WIP tracking and the manpower loop — the original brief, and the part that closes the plan-versus-actual cycle. Material, quality, machines and cost extend it; they do not complete it. If the programme has to stop or pause anywhere, the end of Phase 4 is the place.
